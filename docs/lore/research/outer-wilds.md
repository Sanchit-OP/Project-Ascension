# Outer Wilds (and Echoes of the Eye): lore and lore-delivery research report

**How this was researched.** I read the official Outer Wilds Fandom wiki through its API: about 120 pages on characters, locations, Echoes of the Eye, endings and the ship log. I extracted the full slide text of Kelsey Beachum's GDC 2021 talk *Sparking Curiosity-Driven Exploration Through Narrative in Outer Wilds* (she was the game's writer and narrative designer). I also used interviews with Kelsey and Alex Beachum, Game Developer write-ups of the GDC 2020 talks by Alex Beachum and Loan Verneau, Mobius patch notes, Wikipedia, a critical essay, and Reddit and Steam lore threads. Everything is paraphrased. There are two short direct quotes from the game, both marked.

## PART A: The lore (the "what")

### 1. The Nomai

**Who they were.** The Nomai were a nomadic, very advanced species. They were tall and digitigrade, with goat-like faces, three eyes, three-fingered hands, fur, short tails and antler-like horns. The concept artist described them as a mix of sloths, deer and goats. Their bones were very fragile. A Nomai skeleton in the Hearthian museum is the evidence that Hearthians are not descended from them.

Nearly every Nomai is named after a plant: Solanum, Yarrow, Mallow, Privet, Ilex and so on. Every Hearthian is named after a rock or mineral: Hornfels, Feldspar, Gabbro, Slate and so on.

**Society.** The species was organised into **clans**. Each clan lived aboard its own **Vessel**, an intergalactic ship powered by an Advanced Warp Core, and wandered the universe exploring. Every ten years the clans met at a **festival**. Each clan sent an envoy to present what it had discovered since the last festival. Thatch, one of the survivors, had been Escall's clan envoy. Discoveries such as Annona's warp-core designs were shared freely between clans.

Their philosophy has two tenets, which Solanum names with the symbols "Identify" and "Explain". In her words, *"to seek out and to understand is our way of living."* (quote 1 of 2)

**Core values.** Curiosity and openness about failure. Firm non-interference with, and protection of, less-developed life. They would rather relocate a mine than disturb primitive creatures. Even so, they reached a point where curiosity overrode their ethics (see the Sun Station).

**Writing.** Nomai write by "typing" on a staff or sceptre and pressing its end against a surface. The result is glowing **branching spirals**. Each new branch is usually a different person replying, so a wall reads like a threaded conversation; Kelsey Beachum called it "an ancient email thread." Text sent over a distance shows in a different colour. Children's handwriting is rougher.

The voice is precise and formal but warm. It uses a lot of parenthetical qualifiers and humour. For example, Coleus says the chance of failure is "(not insignificant)".

**Origin of their presence here: Escall's Vessel.**
- Escall was captain and clan leader. Bridge officers Filix and Annona picked up a signal that appeared to be **older than the universe itself**.
- Filix wanted to delay long enough to broadcast a message to the other clans. Escall overruled her, fearing the signal could vanish, and ordered an immediate warp. Annona, who designed the warp core, warned that the core would need time to recharge afterwards.
- The Vessel came out of warp not at the source but **inside Dark Bramble**. The vines fused with the hull, the warp core was destroyed and communications failed.
- Only three of its five escape pods launched. Filix managed to send a distress call before fleeing.
- Because Escall left no message, the Vessel's disappearance became a **myth** among the clans that were still alive. Hundreds of thousands of years later, modern Nomai still tell it as a children's story.
- Many Nomai died. Partners were separated: Kousa never learned what happened to Foli, and Plume's partner Keek and his brother were lost.

**The three escape pods, each its own story arc.**
- **Pod 3**, the first to launch, carried Escall, Secca, Din and others. It crashed inside Dark Bramble, and the pod's scan told them not to leave it. Short on air and harried by anglerfish, they tried to get back to the Vessel. Dark Bramble's space-folding made the Vessel's beacon appear in **two places**. They chose the nearer one, which turned out to be a seed too small to enter. They left final recordings and died one by one around it; this is the "Nomai Grave". Secca's last recorded wish was that the other pods had reached safety.
- **Pod 2** carried Annona, Bur, Coleus, Melorae, Rhus and others. It landed on Ember Twin, which was too hot on the surface. Melorae and Coleus found a cool cavern, and they built the **Sunless City** there.
- **Pod 1**, the last to launch, carried Filix, Kousa, Plume, Thatch and others. It landed on Brittle Hollow. They first built a temporary **Old Settlement** under the crust to shelter from the volcanic moon Hollow's Lantern. Plume argued that to find the Eye they had to "thrive, not merely survive." They then moved north along gravity-crystal paths and built the **Hanging City** beneath the northern glacier.
- Both surviving groups built **Gravity Cannons** to launch shuttles: early interplanetary travel before warp was reinvented. The groups eventually reunited, and later used **projection stones** to talk across planets.

### 2. What the Nomai built, location by location

The Nomai storyline runs as one chain. They searched for the Eye's signal and failed. They studied the Quantum Moon, which taught them what the Eye is and roughly where it orbits. They reinvented warp travel and discovered by accident that it lets them send things back in time. That led to the **Ash Twin Project**, a 22-minute time loop that would let one probe be fired again and again until it found the Eye.

- **The Vessel (Dark Bramble).** The wrecked origin point. It holds Filix's distress message and recent messages from living clans. Its warp core is dead. Restoring power with the Ash Twin Project's core and entering the probe's coordinates gets you to the Eye.
- **Ember Twin.**
  - Escape Pod 2.
  - The **Sunless City**, with four districts: Anglerfish Overlook, Stepping Stone (children's homes), the High Energy Lab Trailhead, and an Eye Shrine. Its records cover the Sun Station debate and the Eye's origin.
  - The **anglerfish fossil**, with the children's game and the finding that anglerfish are blind.
  - The **Quantum Caves**, home of the Cave Shard and Coleus's disappearance.
  - The **Quantum Moon Locator**, a Gravity Cannon, and the **High Energy Lab**, where Pye and Ramie confirmed the negative time interval and designed the Ash Twin warp towers.
  - Chert's astronomy camp is also here.
- **Ash Twin.**
  - Equatorial **warp towers**, each shaped to reflect its destination. A Nomai note grumbles that the sun is not technically a planet.
  - The sealed **Ash Twin Project** in the core: a spinning ring around the Advanced Warp Core and eight mask-storage units. Three masks are live: the player's, Gabbro's and the Probe Tracking Module's. Wall text gives the project's order of events and ends with "Sun Station failed" and "Arrival of comet".
  - It can only be reached through a sand-buried tower, and only while Ember Twin is directly overhead.
- **Brittle Hollow.**
  - Escape Pod 1 and the Old Settlement, with murals of the crash.
  - The **Hanging City**, with four districts: Black Hole Forge, Eye Shrine, Meltwater, and a School District full of children's essays.
  - The **Black Hole Forge**, where every warp core was made from the planet's black hole.
  - The **Tower of Quantum Knowledge**, the final pilgrimage step, which teaches the sixth-location rule.
  - The **Southern Observatory**, a second and more advanced Eye locator, which also fails. Mallow's probe idea and Conoy's link to the time research happen here. Spire's model reveals Giant's Deep's reverse cyclone.
  - A Gravity Cannon linked to Solanum's shuttle, Riebeck's camp, and the **White Hole Station** beyond the black hole. That station was the first rebuilt warp and is where arrivals were first measured *before* departures.
- **Giant's Deep.**
  - The **Orbital Probe Cannon** in orbit, which breaks apart at every launch. Its Probe Tracking Module sinks to the core and holds the Eye's coordinates, found by probe number **9,318,054**.
  - The **Construction Yard** island, where Cassava, Daz, Mallow and Avens died together.
  - **Statue Island**, with Phlox's workshop.
  - The **Tower of Quantum Trials**, hidden in a permanent polar cyclone, which teaches the quantum-imaging rule.
  - Cyclone shelters on every island.
- **Timber Hearth.** The Nomai **mines** where ore for the supernova-proof shell was dug (it was heat-tested in Hollow's Lantern's lava). A "First Encounter" mural shows two suited Nomai watching four-eyed creatures climb out of a pool: the Hearthians' ancestors. There is also a warp receiver for the ore run. The village museum holds a Nomai statue and skeleton.
- **The Attlerock.** The first and simplest **Eye Signal Locator**. It locks onto the Sun, Giant's Deep and Brittle Hollow, but spins aimlessly when asked for the Eye. This is usually the player's first sight of the word "Eye".
- **The Interloper.** A comet with a frozen Nomai shuttle on its dark side; it can be recalled to Ember Twin's cannon, with Clary's body inside. Its melting interior leads to the **ruptured core**, with Poke and Pye's bodies and their last recording.
- **Dark Bramble.** Pod 3, the Nomai Grave, and the Vessel. It is also home to the anglerfish and to the lost Hearthian Feldspar.
- **Quantum Moon and the Sixth Location.** The moon occupies six orbits at once: five around planets and one around the Eye. The pilgrimage teaches three rules: imaging (a photo counts as observation), entanglement (in darkness you move with the object), and the north-pole rule for reaching the sixth location.
  - Solanum's shuttle landed on the south pole. In the first five locations her corpse lies there.
  - At the **sixth location**, which orbits the Eye far from the blast, she is **alive**. Because the moon was in all six places when the comet burst, she died in five of them and survived in one.
  - She speaks to the player by raising symbol stones. She suspects she may not be "entirely alive", admires the player's four eyes, and asks the player to consider her a friend.
- **Sun Station.** A two-module station in a tight orbit around the sun. It fired and failed. It has been in sleep mode for **281,042 years**, and the reddening sun destroys it about 10.5 minutes into each loop.

### 3. What ended them, and the irony

The Sun Station failed: firing it caused only surface flickers, and the Ash Twin Project was paused. Spire then spotted a comet, the **Interloper**, and the Nomai happily set their failure aside to go and study something new. Pye, Poke and Clary flew out to it. Inside they found a stone shell holding a lethal exotic substance under enormous pressure, which would fill the whole system the moment it broke open. Pye sent Poke running to warn the others. The shell ruptured as the comet neared the sun, and **ghost matter** filled the system almost instantly, killing every Nomai at once.

Ghost matter is invisible to the eye but shows up on camera. Its crystals still lie everywhere. It is neutralised under water. The wiki suggests that is why the Hearthians' semi-aquatic ancestors survived, though that is a community inference.

The ironies stack up:
- The Nomai held a firm ethic against endangering life, and anguished over a sun-killing machine that turned out to be harmless. What killed them was their own curiosity: an eager trip to a strange comet.
- The supernova they could not produce happened anyway, from old age, about 281,000 years later. Stars across the universe are dying at once; the universe itself is ending.
- That natural supernova powers their machine. The loop restarts every 22 minutes until probe 9,318,054 finds the Eye.
- At that moment the memory statues, built to pair with "whoever is nearest" once the project succeeds, find no living Nomai. They pair with a young Hearthian in a museum and with Gabbro on an island. The player's time loop *is* the Nomai's experiment, still running.
- Ramie had remarked that it was a comfort the statues would not pair until success, because living through the loop would be hard. Phlox added a fail-safe so that nobody would be stuck unknowingly in a broken loop.
- The player finishes the Nomai journey on their behalf, using the Nomai's own tools, Vessel and coordinates.

### 4. The Hearthians and the travellers

**Hearthians.** Blue, four-eyed and long-eared; their ears droop with age. They have a genderless culture, live in one close-knit wooden village on Timber Hearth, and have a newish space program, **Outer Wilds Ventures**. It was founded by Feldspar, Gossan, Slate and Hornfels, and its look is roughly "1960s NASA meets backpacking". Much of their tech was reverse-engineered from Nomai ruins: artificial gravity, the scout launcher's warp core.

The player is "the Hatchling", the newest recruit. They camp out the night before launch, which is the tradition, and are the first astronaut to carry the **Nomai translator**, which they co-invented with their friend Hal. Hornfels is ground control and runs the museum. Slate is the reckless mechanic. Gossan is the flight instructor.

**Travellers.** Each plays an instrument on a shared frequency so the others can find them with the Signalscope. The instruments were made by Gneiss, and every traveller plays the same campfire melody on their own instrument.
- **Esker** (whistling, the Attlerock): lonely, forgotten by ground control, and secretly spies on the village with scout probes.
- **Riebeck** (banjo, Brittle Hollow): an archaeologist who is terrified of space and got there by tripping over a gravity crystal. Lives for Nomai history.
- **Chert** (drums, Ember Twin): an astronomer who notices too many supernovae. Over each loop Chert realises all the stars are dying and ends up **catatonic**.
- **Gabbro** (flute, Giant's Deep): laid-back, lives in a hammock, and is **also in the loop**. Knows it, is at peace with it, and teaches the player to meditate (which ends a loop early).
- **Feldspar** (harmonica, Dark Bramble): the legendary first pilot, missing for years. Camps inside a dead anglerfish's skeleton in Dark Bramble and is happy not to be "the best that ever was" for a while. Their notes about a frozen jellyfish are the key to reaching Giant's Deep's core.
- **Solanum** (Nomai) and **the Prisoner** (Echoes of the Eye) join only if the player has met them.

How they tie in: the travellers' locations are the soft starting "rumours". Their music is the navigation system. Their emotional states mirror the theme, as with Chert's despair and Gabbro's acceptance. The museum statue and the translator start the plot.

### 5. The ending and its meaning

1. The player takes the Advanced Warp Core out of the Ash Twin Project, which ends the loop (death becomes permanent), and installs it in the Vessel.
2. They enter the probe's coordinates, three glyphs, and warp to the **Eye**.
3. On the Eye they walk through a quantum storm and dive into a maelstrom. They land in a quantum copy of the museum whose plaques now describe what they discovered, then in a forest glade.
4. They light a campfire and track down quantum versions of each traveller by their music. Each conversation reflects that character's arc.
5. Together they play the full theme. A sphere of every possible outcome forms above the fire.
6. The player, a conscious observer, enters it, collapses the possibilities into one, and a **new Big Bang** happens.
7. After the credits, **14.3 billion years later**, a new planet appears. Depending on what the player found, there are insect-like aliens roasting marshmallows at a campfire (if Solanum was met), a blue pool (if the Prisoner was met), and a lantern-carrying creature that looks like a deep-sea fish (if both were met).

**Meaning.**
- The universe was always going to end; the player cannot save it. Kelsey Beachum said the team never considered letting the player save the world, and that the ending works because it is not entirely about the player.
- What the player *can* do is make sure the end is a beginning, so that the universe does not end in nothing. She describes herself as a "cheerful nihilist" and calls joy the antidote to melancholy.
- The Eye seems to be the universe's reset mechanism. It answers to conscious observation, which is presumably why it "calls" to intelligent species near the end.
- The three storylines meet at that one moment: the Nomai's past, the player's present, and the universe's death.

### 6. Echoes of the Eye (DLC, 2021)

**Who the Strangers were.** An unnamed species; fans call them "Owlks". They looked like owl-like birds with antlers, hooves and eyes that reflect light. Their culture centred on **light and visions**, and they rarely wrote anything down.

**Their history.**
- On their home moon (which orbited a gas giant), one of them picked up the Eye's signal **first**, long before the Nomai.
- To reach it they **stripped their home moon bare** to build **the Stranger**: a cloaked, rotating ringworld with a swamp, an endless circular river and a dam.
- At the Eye, a vision device showed them that interacting with it would turn every world to dust. Horrified, they burned one Eye temple and abandoned the other.
- They built a **signal blocker** in orbit around the Eye. This is why the Nomai locators wobbled and failed. They then parked the ship, cloaked, in the Hearthian system. They had been there before the Nomai arrived, and the Nomai never noticed them.
- Grieving their ruined home, they built a **simulation (the Dreamworld)** of it. Through experiments they found the trigger for entering it: fall asleep (or die) by a green fire while holding a specially focused lantern, the "Artifact". Then they all went to sleep inside it.
- **One of them woke up and switched the blocker off.** In that brief window, Escall's Vessel heard the signal. The Prisoner's act of defiance is the reason the Nomai ever came here.
- The others locked that individual in a **vault**, submerged it in the reservoir, sealed it three times inside the simulation, switched the blocker back on, and **burned its controls**.
- They uploaded their slide reels into the simulation and burned the real ones: the frames showing the home moon's destruction, the blocker and the simulation. They scratched the Prisoner's face out of a portrait and burned the Prisoner's dream-house.
- Their bodies have since died. Their minds remain inside the simulation and cannot wake.

**How the player gets there.** A new museum exhibit points to a strange photo from the Hearthian **Deep Space Satellite**: an invisible object blocking sunlight. Going to the satellite at the right inclination reveals the ship's silhouette.

**Slide reels and projectors.** Every settlement has a slide theatre. Reels are stop-motion picture stories with **no words**. Burned or damaged reels have missing frames, and the gaps themselves are clues. Each **Forbidden Archive** inside the simulation holds unburned reels, including **"bug reports"** in which the Strangers document glitches in their own simulation:
- jumping off a raft between areas drops you below the world;
- dying while holding a lantern lets you enter the simulation and keeps you from being woken;
- walking away from your lantern shows hidden wireframe bridges.

The player uses those glitches to break into the vault.

**The Prisoner.** Silent. They show the player their story with a vision torch and then hand it over, so the player can show them what happened next. The Nomai were drawn in, and the player's own journey followed. The Prisoner howls, bows, and walks into the water to let their lantern go out. At the final campfire they ask whether the player is willing to remember them, given the fear of their kind. If welcomed, they join the song.

**How its delivery differs.** It is wordless: slide reels and visions instead of text, and the translator returns an "unknown language" error. Hal jokes that he would need a hundred samples, a codex and six months. It is horror-tinged, with stealth in the dark from "Pursuers". Information is deliberately *censored* in-world, so the act of hiding the truth is part of the story. Its theme is the foil to the Nomai: a species that met the truth and chose darkness and denial, against one that chose curiosity. The pitch was described as a ghost story told around a campfire.

## PART B: How the lore is delivered (the "how")

### 7. Knowledge as progression; the Ship Log

**Design intent.** Alex Beachum's master's thesis defined the goal as a game that "inspires and rewards curiosity". Curiosity-driven exploration means choosing to explore mainly to understand. Kelsey Beachum's GDC talk lists what Outer Wilds deliberately leaves out: XP, new abilities, items, upgrades, cosmetics, newly unlocked areas, relationship progress, scores and collectibles.

**Knowledge is the only reward, and the only form of progression.** In theory a player who knows the solution could finish in one loop. The team admitted this openly and built their protections around knowledge rather than locks. They accepted that this would put off achiever-type players, and chose to serve explorers fully instead.

Three design pillars, from Alex Beachum:
1. curiosity-driven exploration;
2. a world that changes outside the player's control (they deliberately chose that the player cannot affect the sand flow);
3. the feel of camping in space, cosy but vulnerable.

He describes the story as **four main curiosities** that answer the big questions, each needing about three clues to reach.

The **time loop** makes the world ignore the player. Death becomes punctuation rather than punishment, and memory is the only thing that carries over.

**The Ship Log** is the computer on the player's ship.
- **Map Mode** sorts entries by planet and location.
- **Rumour Mode** shows entries as cards with lines connecting related discoveries. Places the player has only heard about appear as rumour cards pointing outwards. The layout differs from player to player, depending on the order they found things.
- Threads are colour-coded, so players can follow one mystery.
- A green "!" marks unread entries. An orange "*" marks a location with more to find. Any entry can be pinned to the HUD as a navigation marker.

The key writing rule, from the GDC slides: log entries record **only what the player has 100% certainly seen**. There are no leaps of logic. The *player* does the inference and the log does the bookkeeping, because remembering what you have learned should never be the hard part. A wiki note says the log keeps its contents across loops because it runs on a fragment of a Nomai statue; treat that as a community reading.

### 8. Delivery methods and why each exists

- **Nomai wall spirals** (the main channel). The text is physically in the world, so there is room for it and it becomes a place. The translator turns reading into an action: you aim it and sweep each branch.
  - The spiral form gives a **branching conversation**, and each speaker's personality shows through.
  - Kelsey's rules of thumb: the **longest branch carries the most important information**, and **branch endings (sometimes beginnings) carry an actionable clue**.
  - Text you have not read yet looks different from text you have, so it is clear what is left.
  - She designed every text to point backwards (where to learn what came before) and forwards (where to go next). Her worked example is a four-part Ramie and Pye exchange on the High Energy Lab door. It explains that the door is shut because of an experiment, alludes to the White Hole Station, and tells you the lab is now reached only from the Sunless City.
- **Recorders and computers.** Last words and machine readouts: Escape Pod 3's final messages, the Interloper core recording, the Sun Station's countdown, the probe log stating which probe found the Eye, the Ash Twin Project's "received data from previous cycles". These give terse, dated, factual punches that contrast with the chatty walls.
- **Scrolls.** Portable text in sockets, often hidden in a side room as a reward. Example: the Sunless City scroll that reveals the warp-tower route to the Sun Station.
- **Projection stones and pools.** Stones replay message archives between two linked walls, with remote messages in orange. This turns the Nomai's long-distance working relationships into readable correspondence. Pools give a live 3D view of a linked place, which works as a direct hint to its location.
- **Memory statues.** They deliver no text. Instead they *are* the mechanism of the plot: the opening flashback at the museum is the hook for "why am I looping?".
- **Murals and environment.** The Old Settlement's crash murals and the First Encounter mural. Skeletons posed where people died: Idaea facing the sun, the circle around the Nomai Grave seed, Poke one step from the exit. Spaces built by children, like the hole sized for small kids at the Anglerfish Overlook.
- **Hearthian NPCs.** Hornfels's opening dialogue menu gives a parallel "where should I go?" menu: learn about the Nomai, meet the travellers, go somewhere new, or start small, with the Attlerock as the gentle default. Village gossip gives the soft hooks.
- **Signals.** The travellers' music, distress beacons and quantum signals make sound a navigation layer. The Signalscope's hide-and-seek game teaches it in a playful way.
- **Slide reels and vision torches** (Echoes of the Eye). Wordless pictures for a culture built around light.

### 9. Non-linearity without breaking the mystery

- **No gating, no funnels.** Kelsey says the "string of pearls" model could not work. They had to assume the player might know *any* combination of facts at any time, anywhere from only what is needed to reach space to everything except the ending. Tailoring text to every possible path was impossible.
- **Lots of small, self-contained arcs** that build on each other in any order. Her example is the Nomai arrival, told as separate arcs: the Vessel, Pod 1, Pod 2, Pod 3 and the reunion. Each is satisfying alone, and they add up regardless of order.
- **Three depths of content.**
  - *Surface* text is literally on planet surfaces. It is easy to find and raises questions.
  - *Mid-level* text is usually found by following clues, but can be stumbled on. It moves a thread forward and says where to learn more.
  - *Hidden* text needs specific knowledge to reach, and **answers to the mysteries live only here**. That is the main protection against sequence breaks: early finds raise questions rather than answer them.
- **Time and physics as soft gates** instead of keys. Ember Twin's caves fill with sand from Ash Twin. Brittle Hollow crumbles and can drop the Tower of Quantum Knowledge into the black hole and out at White Hole Station. The Interloper's ice melts open as it nears the sun. Giant's Deep throws islands into space. The Sun Station burns up at about 10.5 minutes. Alex Beachum's "4D level design" talk covers designing for *when* as well as *where*.
- **Rumours as pull.** Places appear in the log, in conversations and in Nomai text *before* the player arrives, so every location is wrapped in questions first. Their GDC 2020 talk recalled a failed prototype in which a meteor crashed into the village: players were not curious, because you cannot be curious about something new until the place around it is familiar.
- **Redundancy.** Important facts appear in several places:
  - The **Eye** shows up on the Attlerock, at the Southern Observatory, in both cities' Eye Shrines, in the escape pods, and through Solanum.
  - **Anglerfish are blind** shows up in the fossil research, the children's game, a Nomai debate about retrieving the Vessel's core, and Feldspar's notes.
  - The **Ash Twin Project** is described at the High Energy Lab, the Sunless City, the Hanging City, the Construction Yard, the Sun Station and the project itself.
  - The **quantum rules** are spread across all the shards and towers, plus Gabbro's musings and a museum plaque.
- **The final requirement is split into three deep discoveries**: the probe coordinates in Giant's Deep's core, the warp core in Ash Twin, and the Vessel in Dark Bramble. Even if you blunder into one early, you lack the context for the others.
- **Known weak point.** Beachum has said the design does not push back as hard as he would like against players who "clear" planets one at a time instead of following questions. The colour coding and rumour arrows are the counterweight. (This comes from a secondary interview; see §13.)

### 10. Pacing reveals and making players care about a dead species

- **Push vs pull.** Forced text is limited to the very start and the very end of the game. Everything else is *available*, its availability is signalled, and the player chooses when to take it. Kelsey reports that players took anywhere from 30 minutes to 2–3 hours before reading their first Nomai text, and that this was fine: the story waits until the player is ready.
- **The big hooks are shown, not told.** They are things the player sees: the probe launch exploding in the sky at the start of every loop, the cyclones, the giant ruins, and above all the sun exploding. Text rarely delivers the first hook.
- **People rather than a civilisation.** It is easier to connect with individuals than with a group, and the Nomai had to be people players *want to spend time with*. Kelsey iterated heavily on their voice: "we" versus "I", aiming for empathy rather than sympathy, different from the Hearthian voice, formal without being stiff, and funny.
- **Ordinary detail first, cosmic stakes later.** Early material is children's games, a festival essay, a pun, a date, a sibling feud, someone wanting to build a model. Kelsey deliberately mixed light, silly arcs in among the devastating ones, and she says the Nomai's comfort with failure reinforces failure as progress. By the time the player reads Poke's last run or Secca's last wish, they know these people.
- **Moral conflict written as debate.** The Sun Station argument is staged as a wall conversation. Kelsey insisted on keeping the Nomai consistent with their own values. She justified the reckless choice through character: the older survivors wanted their dead to have died for something.
- **Growing relevance.** Nomai text starts out as archaeology and gradually becomes *about the player*. The loop is theirs, the statues paired with the player, and the player completes their journey.

### 11. Lore and mechanics are the same thing

Almost every key fact is also a piece of traversal or puzzle knowledge.

| Lore fact | What it teaches the player to do |
|---|---|
| The children's anglerfish game: the fish are blind | Drift silently past anglerfish in Dark Bramble. |
| Coleus's disappearance on the wandering rock | Quantum entanglement: stand on a quantum object in darkness and you move with it. That is how the Quantum Shrine works. |
| Tower of Quantum Trials | Quantum imaging: photograph the Quantum Moon to land on it. |
| Bells's note to Solanum at the Tower of Quantum Knowledge | Use the shrine at the moon's north pole to reach the sixth location. |
| Spire and Conoy's cyclone model | One cyclone spins the other way and pushes things *down*; ride it through Giant's Deep's current. |
| Feldspar's frozen jellyfish | Jellyfish insulate against the core's electrical field; get inside one. |
| Escape Pod 3's duplicate beacons and the Nomai Grave | Their fatal dead end becomes the player's route: fire a scout into the seed and follow the duplicate signal to the Vessel. |
| Pye and Ramie's door note; the Sunless City scroll | The real routes to the High Energy Lab and the Sun Station. |
| White Hole Station and Clary/Poke's alignment notes | How warp towers work, which is how to reach the Ash Twin Project, the Forge and the Sun Station. |
| Negative time interval at the High Energy Lab | The player can reproduce it, and even cause a paradox that ends the game. |
| Gabbro's calm | The meditation option, which ends a loop early. |
| Echoes of the Eye "bug reports" | The three glitches the player exploits to open the vault. |

Kelsey's slides frame the translator itself as "gameplay and narrative design working together": reading became a physical act in the world.

### 12. Techniques worth stealing

- **Write each text as a node with outgoing links.** Every inscription should either say where to go next or say where the earlier part happened. *Example:* the High Energy Lab door note mentions White Hole Station and gives the Sunless City route.
- **Grade content into surface, mid and hidden, and put answers only in the hidden layer.** *Example:* the Attlerock locator asks the question and the Ash Twin Project answers it.
- **Run the log as a ledger of facts, not a quest log.** Record only what the player has certainly seen and let them infer the rest. *Example:* Ship Log entries never state a conclusion before you have read its proof.
- **Show unexplored places as rumour cards.** *Example:* the cards in Rumour Mode, and Hornfels's "no one has landed on the Interloper" line.
- **Give the most important information the most physical weight.** The main line of the story goes in the longest branch, and the next step in the branch ending. *Example:* the Nomai spiral layout.
- **Make the extinction readable through bodies and poses.** *Example:* Idaea facing the sun, the ring around the Nomai Grave, Poke one step from the exit.
- **Tell the arrival as several parallel survival arcs**, each complete on its own. *Example:* the three escape pods.
- **Stage ethics as an argument between named people.** *Example:* Idaea against Pye over the Sun Station; Spire against Mitis over recovering the Vessel's core.
- **Write the ancients as coworkers, families and children.** *Example:* Mallow and Avens ignoring the power cap, Clary and Yarrow's date, Cycad's puns, the children's essays and games.
- **Turn every lore fact into a verb.** *Example:* blindness becomes stealth; Coleus becomes the entanglement rule.
- **Let a tragedy become the path forward.** *Example:* Escape Pod 3's duplicate beacon leads the player to the Vessel.
- **Let time and dynamic systems do the gating** instead of locked doors. *Example:* sand covering the High Energy Lab trail; Brittle Hollow collapsing.
- **Make the player's mechanic part of the ancients' plot.** *Example:* the time loop is the Nomai's experiment, and the statue paired with the player only because no Nomai were left.
- **Let world text change with what the player knows.** *Example:* museum plaques change depending on whether the cause of the Nomai's death has been found, and the quantum museum at the Eye reflects all your discoveries.
- **Use a living "last one"** to give the dead civilisation a voice and a quiet emotional payoff. *Example:* Solanum at the sixth location.
- **Let a second civilisation explain an anomaly in the first.** *Example:* the Strangers' blocker explains why the Nomai locators failed, and the Prisoner's act explains why the Nomai came at all.
- **Use censorship as storytelling.** *Example:* burned slides, the scratched-out portrait, archives hidden inside the dream.
- **Use sound and music as a navigation and companionship layer.** *Example:* travellers' instruments on a shared frequency, all coming together in the finale.
- **Show the big hooks spectacularly.** *Example:* the probe exploding at loop start, the sun going supernova.
- **Use the dialogue menu as a compass for players who want direction.** *Example:* Hornfels's "what's your plan?" options.
- **Leave room for humour and small easter eggs** to balance the melancholy. *Example:* Pye's mission statement, and the tower trial that admits the last test was made harder.

Sources: as for §§7–11.

### 13. Criticisms, weaknesses, and what the DLC changed

- **Heavy on text.** Some critics argue the game *tells* too much, with Nomai text reading like meeting notes, when its environments and systems could have shown it. The essay "Outer Wilds Didn't Need a Writer" makes this case, including against the Tower of Quantum Trials explaining its rule in words.
- **Getting lost and navigating late in the game.** Reviewers noted confusion late in the game. Kelsey admits players take 30 minutes to 3 hours to start reading. Beachum says the structure does not stop players clearing planets one by one; this comes from a secondary interview (Medium) I could not open directly, so treat it as less verified.
- **Difficulty spikes and "cheap" deaths** that wipe a loop: anglerfish, Dark Bramble, Brittle Hollow falls, Giant's Deep. Game Informer, GameSpot and Polygon all flagged it.
- **Self-selected audience.** The team knowingly gave up on achiever players.
- **Echoes of the Eye.**
  - Its wordless slide reels and visual storytelling were praised as the counterpoint to the base game's text-heaviness.
  - The horror and stealth sections were criticised, by GameSpot most sharply. A later patch added a "Reduced Frights" option, which some players felt did not touch the scariest parts.
  - Most importantly for us, the wordless delivery made players miss threads. Mobius patched the expansion twice:
    - Patch 1.1.12 made "significant revisions to several mid-game clues", updated slide reels and Ship Log entries, and added visuals to make the ending feel final.
    - Patch 13 said the expansion "no longer requires a photographic memory", revised visual clues and Ship Log entries, made the conclusion "significantly less subtle", and reworked two dream areas.

  The lesson is that wordless delivery gains atmosphere at the cost of legibility, and it needed the Ship Log, recaps and stronger cues to work. (These short phrases are patch-note wording quoted by the fetch tool, not quotes from the game.)

## Short note on what this means for Project-Ascension

Outer Wilds lines up closely with the Ascension premise: a vanished multi-world civilisation, a gateway network, and something that ended them.

- **Inscriptions as nodes.** Make each Archive inscription a node with outgoing links, using the surface/mid/hidden grading.
- **Gateways as the Nomai's warp towers.** Each frame's attunement rules should be learned from the ancients' own work notes.
- **Bodies as the story of the ending.** Let posed remains and parallel survivor arcs (one per world) tell how it ended.
- **A fact-only Ship Log.** If we build one, it should record only what the player has certainly seen and show unvisited worlds as rumours.
- **One mechanic tied to their plot.** Make at least one of the player's core mechanics turn out to be the ancients' unfinished project.

Watch the Echoes of the Eye lesson as well. Wordless or highly atmospheric delivery, which suits Minecraft's limited text space, needs stronger cues and a log behind it, or players lose threads.
